\section*{Executive Summary}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Executive Summary}

Airlines typically spend months analysing which routes to keep, expand, or cut. This project asks a more tractable question: given everything we know about a route \emph{before} it operates, can we predict whether it will turn a profit?

We applied five machine learning classifiers -- Random Forest, XGBoost, Support Vector Machine, Logistic Regression with polynomial features, and a Multi-Layer Perceptron -- to a dataset of 15,000 international airline routes. The dataset combined a public Kaggle source (Waleed Faheem, 7,974 records) with 18 operationally motivated synthetic features covering competition, weather disruption, and aircraft characteristics, plus a binary target derived from profit margin.

All five models outperformed a Decision Tree baseline. The MLP achieved the highest F1 score (0.937) and the best Matthews Correlation Coefficient (0.827). The SVM produced the lowest estimated false positive cost (£1.76M on the test set), making it the preferred option in scenarios where approving a loss-making route carries a high financial penalty. Load factor and price per kilometre emerged as the two most consistently predictive features across all methods.

The results support deploying an ML-assisted screening tool in the route planning workflow, with the specific model choice depending on whether planners prioritise raw predictive accuracy or minimising the approval of unprofitable routes.

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
% ============================================================

\subsection{Business context and motivation}
\label{subsec:context}

Commercial aviation runs on thin margins. According to IATA, the average net profit per passenger was around \$6--8 in 2023 -- a figure that evaporates quickly if a route consistently flies with half its seats empty or faces unexpected fuel cost spikes. Airline network planners decide each scheduling cycle which routes to operate, modify, or drop, and they typically do this using historical load data combined with analyst intuition. The process is slow and inconsistent.

The business case for a machine learning approach is straightforward: if a classifier can identify likely loss-making routes before they are scheduled, planners can redirect aircraft and crew resources to routes with better financial prospects. The cost of a wrong approval -- committing an aircraft and crew to an unprofitable service -- runs to tens of thousands of pounds in direct operating costs per route. Getting those decisions systematically right compounds over hundreds of routes per season.

This project builds a binary classifier trained on pre-flight observable route characteristics. The output is a probability score and a final prediction: profitable or not. A secondary goal is to understand which features drive the prediction, so that network planners can also act on the model's logic, not just its output.

\subsection{Problem statement}
\label{subsec:problem}

Given a set of features observable before a route operates -- aircraft type, origin and destination, expected load factor, ticket pricing, fuel price, competition index, seasonality, and operational characteristics -- predict whether the route will generate positive profit margin.

Formally, let $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^{40}$ be a feature vector for route $i$, and $y_i \in \{0, 1\}$ the label where $y_i = 1$ denotes a profitable route. The task is to learn $f: \mathbb{R}^{40} \to \{0, 1\}$ such that $f(\mathbf{x}_i) = y_i$ on unseen data.

We define a route as profitable if its \texttt{Profit\_Margin} is positive after all direct operating costs (fuel, crew, maintenance, airport fees, etc.). This definition is applied consistently to both training and evaluation.

\subsection{Research questions}
\label{subsec:rq}

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
  \item Which pre-flight features are most consistently predictive of route profitability across different algorithm families?
  \item Among the five classifiers tested, which offers the best tradeoff between false positive rate (approving a loss-making route) and overall predictive accuracy?
  \item How much performance variation exists across algorithm families when applied to the same data and evaluation protocol?
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Assumptions}
\label{subsec:assumptions}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item All features used in modelling must be observable \emph{before} a route is scheduled. Post-flight financial outcomes (realised revenue, realised fuel cost) are excluded to prevent data leakage.
  \item A false positive (classifying a loss-making route as profitable) costs an estimated £42,000 in wasted operational investment. A false negative (rejecting a profitable route) costs £30,000 in missed revenue. These figures are used to compute a financial impact metric for model comparison.
  \item Marks (grades) are not correlated with model performance. The goal is correct methodology and clear justification, not maximising a single number.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Dataset overview}
\label{subsec:dataset}

\subsubsection{Source and provenance}

The base dataset is the \emph{Airline Route Profitability and Cost Analysis} dataset published by Waleed Faheem on Kaggle~\cite{faheem2024kaggle}, containing 7,974 flight records with 33 features covering route characteristics, aircraft type, revenue, and operating costs. We extended this dataset in two ways:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Feature extension}: 18 additional features were added to represent factors not captured in the original data but known to influence route economics -- airline identity and alliance, origin/destination countries, aircraft age, competition index, weather disruption probability, on-time performance, delay statistics, cargo revenue, fuel price per litre, passenger satisfaction score, and market share percentage.
  \item \textbf{Record augmentation}: Records were augmented to 15,000 to provide a larger training corpus, preserving the distributional characteristics of the original data.
\end{enumerate}

The augmentation was motivated by the need for sufficient training data across all five algorithm families, some of which (SVM, MLP) are more data-hungry than others (Decision Tree). The synthetic extension followed the distributional constraints of the original Kaggle data to maintain realism.

The binary target variable \texttt{Profitable} was derived from \texttt{Profit\_Margin}: routes with positive profit margin were labelled 1, all others 0. This gave a class balance of 63.67\% profitable and 36.33\% loss-making across the full dataset -- a mild imbalance that was addressed in several models through class weighting.

\section{Data Understanding, Pre-Processing, and Exploration}

This section covers CRISP-DM phases 2 and 3. All code for this section is in \texttt{Group\_Data\_Understanding\_Preprocessing.ipynb}.

\subsection{Initial data inspection}
\label{subsec:inspection}

The raw dataset loaded as 15,000 rows and 51 columns. Initial inspection confirmed that the target column (\texttt{Profitable}) was present and free of nulls. Three rows had missing values in continuous features and were dropped, leaving 14,997 clean records.

Column types spanned three categories: numerical continuous (\texttt{Load\_Factor}, \texttt{Flight\_Distance\_KM}, \texttt{Fuel\_Price\_Per\_Liter}, etc.), categorical ordinal (\texttt{Season}, \texttt{Demand\_Level}), and categorical nominal (\texttt{Alliance}, \texttt{Route\_Category}, \texttt{Origin\_Country}). Understanding these types upfront was necessary before choosing encoding strategies -- one-hot encoding for nominals, ordinal integer encoding for ordered categories.

Class distribution is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:class_load}. The 63.67\%/36.33\% split is mild enough that most models can handle it without resampling, though several members used class weighting in their hyperparameter search spaces as a deliberate option.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{fig1_class_and_load}
  \caption{Class distribution and load factor by profitability class. Profitable routes have noticeably higher average load factors, which aligned with our initial hypothesis.}
  \label{fig:class_load}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Leakage removal}
\label{subsec:leakage}

Twenty-one columns were removed before any modelling work. These columns represent post-flight financial outcomes: \texttt{Profit}, \texttt{Profit\_Margin}, \texttt{Total\_Revenue}, \texttt{Total\_Cost}, and all individual cost line items (fuel cost, crew cost, catering, etc.). These values are only known once the route has operated, so including them would give the model information it cannot have in a real deployment setting.

This is a critical design decision. A model trained with these features would produce misleadingly high accuracy -- in testing it would correctly identify profitable routes because it could directly infer profit from the revenue and cost columns -- but it would fail entirely when applied to a new, unscheduled route where those columns are empty.

Three identifier columns (\texttt{Flight\_ID}, \texttt{Flight\_Number}, \texttt{Route}) were also removed as they carry no predictive signal. After leakage removal, 27 original features remained.

\subsection{Outlier detection and handling}
\label{subsec:outliers}

Figure~\ref{fig:outliers} shows the outlier detection output using the interquartile range (IQR) method across all continuous features. The most affected features were \texttt{Delay\_Minutes} and \texttt{Average\_Ticket\_Price}, both of which have long right tails from unusually delayed or premium-priced flights.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.90\textwidth]{fig0_outlier_detection}
  \caption{Outlier detection using IQR method across continuous features. Red points indicate values beyond 1.5$\times$IQR from quartile boundaries.}
  \label{fig:outliers}
\end{figure}

Winsorisation at the 5th and 95th percentiles was applied to the three most affected features. Hard removal of outliers was avoided because extreme values can represent genuine business scenarios (e.g.\ a long-haul route with unusually high delays during disruption events) that the model should be able to learn from.

\subsection{Feature engineering}
\label{subsec:feateng}

Six new features were constructed from the original columns:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{\texttt{price\_per\_km}}: Average ticket price divided by flight distance. This ratio captures pricing efficiency more meaningfully than either variable alone -- a high ticket price on a short route signals stronger unit economics than the same price on a 10,000km route.

  \item \textbf{\texttt{load\_revenue\_ratio}}: Load factor multiplied by average ticket price. This product approximates revenue density per seat, combining demand realisation with pricing in a single feature.

  \item \textbf{\texttt{delay\_flag}}: Binary indicator set to 1 when average delay exceeds 30 minutes. Chronic delays correlate with operational cost overruns, making this a useful signal even after continuous \texttt{Delay\_Minutes} is also present.

  \item \textbf{\texttt{flight\_month}}: Extracted from \texttt{Flight\_Date} to capture within-year seasonality at a finer granularity than the existing \texttt{Season} categorical.

  \item \textbf{\texttt{is\_weekend}}: Whether the majority of the route's operations fall on Saturday or Sunday, which affects both demand and staffing costs.

  \item \textbf{\texttt{is\_narrow\_body}}: Binary flag for narrow-body aircraft (A320, B737 family). Narrow-body routes tend to operate differently from wide-body long-haul in terms of cost structure.

  \item \textbf{Cyclical season encoding}: \texttt{Season} was encoded as both an ordinal integer and as sine/cosine components to allow models to capture the circular nature of the seasonal calendar (Peak \textrightarrow{} Shoulder \textrightarrow{} Normal \textrightarrow{} Peak) without imposing a false linear order.

  \item \textbf{Region mapping}: Country-level origin and destination were mapped to seven geographic regions (Europe, Asia, North America, Middle East, South America, Oceania, Other). This reduces high-cardinality country encodings to a tractable set of dummies.
\end{itemize}

Three features with strong right skew -- \texttt{Flight\_Distance\_KM}, \texttt{Average\_Ticket\_Price}, and \texttt{Delay\_Minutes} -- were log-transformed using \texttt{log1p} before scaling. Figure~\ref{fig:log1p} shows the before/after distributions for these features. The transformation reduces the influence of extreme values on distance-sensitive algorithms (particularly SVM with RBF kernel).

\begin{figure}[H]

\end{figure}

Algorithms that are invariant to feature scale (tree-based methods) received scaled features for consistency, though their behaviour does not depend on it.

\section{Modelling}
\label{sec:modelling}
% ============================================================

\subsection{Random Forest -- Gokulkrishna Santhoshkumar}
\label{subsec:rf}

\subsubsection{Algorithm overview}

Random Forest builds an ensemble of decision trees, each trained on a bootstrap sample of the training data~\cite{breiman2001random}. At each split, a random subset of features is considered (controlled by \texttt{max\_features}), which reduces correlation between individual trees and improves generalisation.

For a route profitability classification task, Random Forest has several useful properties: it handles mixed feature types (continuous, binary, ordinal) without needing careful scaling, it is inherently resistant to outliers at the split level, and it produces both class probabilities and a built-in feature importance measure (Mean Decrease in Impurity, MDI). The ensemble's averaging of 200 tree predictions smooths out the variance typical of a single decision tree.

The main weakness of Random Forest in this context is that MDI-based feature importance can overstate the importance of high-cardinality continuous features, because they have more split candidates. This was addressed by computing permutation importance on the test set alongside MDI.

\subsubsection{Decision Tree baseline}

Best parameters: \texttt{max\_depth=7}, \texttt{criterion=gini}, \texttt{min\_samples\_leaf=20}, \texttt{class\_weight=balanced}. The depth constraint was chosen to keep the tree interpretable -- at depth 7, the tree is still visualisable. The tree visualisation is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:dt_tree_rf}.

\subsection{Support Vector Machine -- Apprajit Manikandan}
\label{subsec:svm}

\subsubsection{Algorithm overview}

Support Vector Machine with an RBF (radial basis function) kernel finds the maximum-margin hyperplane in an implicit high-dimensional feature space~\cite{cortes1995support}. The RBF kernel computes similarity between data points based on Euclidean distance in the transformed space; the kernel width parameter \texttt{gamma} controls how far the influence of a single training example reaches. The regularisation parameter \texttt{C} governs the tradeoff between maximising the margin and penalising misclassifications.

SVM was chosen because it makes no distributional assumptions about the input features, handles non-linear decision boundaries through the kernel trick, and tends to produce well-calibrated probability estimates after Platt scaling. It was expected to be competitive on this dataset given the partial class overlap visible in the PCA projection.

SVM requires scaled features for its RBF kernel to function correctly: the kernel computes distances in feature space, so features with larger numerical ranges would dominate unless standardised.

\subsubsection{Decision Tree baseline}

Best DT parameters: \texttt{criterion=entropy}, \texttt{max\_depth=7}, \texttt{min\_samples\_leaf=20}, \texttt{class\_weight=balanced}.

\subsubsection{Hyperparameter tuning}

RandomizedSearchCV over 20 iterations:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*, noitemsep]
  \item \texttt{C}: 1, 10, 50, 100
  \item \texttt{gamma}: 0.01, 0.02, 0.05, 0.1
  \item \texttt{probability}: True (enables Platt scaling for threshold sweep)
\end{itemize}

Best parameters: \texttt{C=1}, \texttt{gamma=0.01}. The low C value imposes strong regularisation, allowing some misclassifications to achieve a wider margin. The wide kernel (\texttt{gamma=0.01}) means each training point influences a large region -- appropriate for a dataset where class boundaries are not sharply defined. Best CV AUC: \textbf{0.9669}. Five-fold CV F1: \textbf{0.9158 $\pm$ 0.0090} (per-fold: 0.9271, 0.9049, 0.9259, 0.9119, 0.9090). Tuning reduced the validation overfit gap from 0.018 (baseline) to 0.003.

\subsection{Logistic Regression with Polynomial Features -- Afzal Hakkim}
\label{subsec:lr}

\subsubsection{Algorithm overview}

Logistic Regression estimates the log-odds of class membership as a linear combination of features~\cite{hosmer2013applied}. On its own it is limited to linear decision boundaries, which the PCA projection in Figure~\ref{fig:pca} suggested would be insufficient. Augmenting the feature space with polynomial expansions (all pairwise interactions and squared terms) allows the model to learn non-linear patterns while retaining the interpretability of a coefficient-per-feature structure.

A degree-2 polynomial expansion over 40 features produces up to $\binom{40+2}{2} - 1 \approx 860$ terms including interaction and squared features. With L2 regularisation (Ridge penalty), most of these additional terms shrink toward zero, and the model effectively selects the interactions that matter.

This approach was chosen to represent the class of interpretable non-linear models. Unlike tree-based methods, each prediction can be traced back to a specific combination of input feature interactions with an explicit coefficient.

\subsubsection{Decision Tree baseline}

Best DT parameters: \texttt{criterion=entropy}, \texttt{max\_depth=7}, \texttt{min\_samples\_leaf=5}.

\subsubsection{Hyperparameter tuning}

GridSearchCV over 30 combinations using a \texttt{Pipeline(PolynomialFeatures \textrightarrow{} StandardScaler \textrightarrow{} LogisticRegression)}:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*, noitemsep]
  \item \texttt{poly\_\_degree}: 1, 2
  \item \texttt{lr\_\_C}: 0.01, 0.1, 1, 10, 100
  \item \texttt{lr\_\_class\_weight}: None, balanced
\end{itemize}

Best parameters: \texttt{degree=2}, \texttt{C=0.1}, \texttt{class\_weight=None}. The moderate regularisation (\texttt{C=0.1}) prevents the polynomial interaction terms from overfitting on sparse feature combinations. Scaling inside the pipeline is essential: the polynomial expansion creates interaction terms with magnitudes far larger than the original features, which without scaling would overwhelm the L2 penalty. Best CV AUC: \textbf{0.9640}. Five-fold CV F1: \textbf{0.9193 $\pm$ 0.0091} (per-fold: 0.9354, 0.9108, 0.9235, 0.9132, 0.9137). The baseline logistic regression (degree=1) achieved near-zero overfit gap (0.0001), meaning it was already fully regularised but underfitting; degree=2 raised both train and validation performance.

\subsection{Multi-Layer Perceptron -- Vino Mohandas Nedumpully}

\subsubsection{Algorithm overview}

A Multi-Layer Perceptron is a fully connected feedforward neural network that learns a hierarchical feature representation through stacked nonlinear transformations~\cite{goodfellow2016deep}. Each hidden layer applies an affine transformation followed by an activation function; the final layer uses a sigmoid output for binary classification.

The MLP was chosen to represent the deep learning family of models. Its key advantage over the other models tested is its capacity to learn arbitrary feature interactions automatically, without requiring manual polynomial expansion or tree-based splits. The main disadvantage is interpretability: unlike the coefficient-based LR model or the visualisable tree, the MLP's predictions cannot be easily attributed to specific feature combinations without additional tools (e.g.\ SHAP).

All inputs were standardised before training, as gradient descent in neural networks is highly sensitive to feature scale differences.

\section{Performance Evaluation}
\label{sec:evaluation}
% ============================================================

\subsection{Evaluation metric justification}
\label{subsec:metrics}

Accuracy alone is a misleading metric for this task. With 63.7\% of routes being profitable, a naive classifier that always predicts "profitable" would achieve 63.7\% accuracy without learning anything. We used four complementary metrics:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{F1-score}: Harmonic mean of precision and recall. Penalises models that trade heavily between the two, which matters when both false positives (wasted resources) and false negatives (missed revenue) carry business costs.

  \item \textbf{ROC-AUC}: Area under the receiver operating characteristic curve. Measures discrimination ability across all classification thresholds, independent of the chosen decision boundary. Useful for comparing models that will be threshold-tuned in deployment.

  \item \textbf{Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC)}: Captures all four cells of the confusion matrix in a single number. Unlike F1, it penalises both directions of error symmetrically and is not inflated by class imbalance. A value of 1 is perfect prediction; 0 is no better than random.

  \item \textbf{FP Cost (£)}: The estimated financial cost of false positives on the 1,500-record test set, computed as (number of FPs) $\times$ £42,000 per route. This directly translates model errors into business impact.
\end{itemize}

Metrics were computed consistently across all five notebooks using the same test set and the same metric functions, allowing direct comparison.

\subsection{Key observations from the comparison}
\label{subsec:observations}

\paragraph{All primary models beat their baselines.} Every primary model improved on its Decision Tree baseline in F1 score, with gains ranging from 0.005 (LR + Poly) to 0.021 (Random Forest). This confirms that the additional model complexity was warranted for each member's chosen algorithm.

\paragraph{The F1 range across primary models is narrow.} The gap between the best (MLP: 0.937) and worst (SVM: 0.928) primary model is only 0.009 F1 points. This suggests the task is well-conditioned and that the major performance driver is data quality and feature engineering rather than algorithm choice.

\paragraph{Model selection depends on the business objective.} On raw F1 and MCC, the MLP and XGBoost are the strongest performers. But on false positive cost, the SVM wins by a significant margin (£1.76M vs the next-best XGBoost at £2.77M). A network planner who wants to minimise the risk of approving unprofitable routes should deploy the SVM; one who wants the highest overall accuracy should use the MLP.

\paragraph{Overfitting is controlled across the board.} All five primary models have overfit gaps below 0.05, with the SVM and MLP showing slightly negative gaps, meaning their test performance marginally exceeds training performance. This pattern is consistent with strong regularisation in both models.

\section{Discussion, Conclusions, and Recommendations}
\label{sec:discussion}
% ============================================================

\subsection{What the models tell us about route profitability}
\label{subsec:insights}

The consistency of \texttt{Load\_Factor} and \texttt{price\_per\_km} across all five feature importance analyses is the clearest finding from this project. These two features -- how full the aircraft is and how much revenue each kilometre of travel generates -- explain more variance in profitability than any other input in the dataset. This is intuitive from a business standpoint: a route running at 85\% load at high per-km yields will almost always be profitable regardless of other factors, while a route running at 65\% load at low yields will struggle to cover operating costs.

\texttt{Competition\_Index} consistently ranked in the top 5 features across tree-based models and appeared as a moderating influence on \texttt{price\_per\_km}: routes in highly competitive markets see their per-km revenue compressed by rival pricing, even when demand is strong. This interaction was captured most clearly in the polynomial logistic regression, where the \texttt{price\_per\_km} $\times$ \texttt{Competition\_Index} interaction term carried a negative coefficient.

Seasonal features contributed meaningfully. Peak season routes are consistently more profitable than shoulder or normal season equivalents, and the cyclical sin/cos encoding outperformed a simple ordinal in the MLP (as measured by held-out validation loss during tuning). Route category -- short haul vs long haul -- also shows up in the upper layers of all tree visualisations, driven partly by the different cost structures of narrow-body vs wide-body operations.

\subsection{Business recommendations}
\label{subsec:recommendations}

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Deploy the SVM as the default screening tool for new route proposals.} Its precision of 0.954 and TNR of 0.923 make it the most reliable option for flagging loss-making routes before they enter the schedule. The £1.76M estimated FP cost on a 1,500-route test sample (equivalent to roughly 42 incorrectly approved routes) translates to around 4 misclassifications per 100 route evaluations.

  \item \textbf{Use the MLP or XGBoost as a secondary validation step.} These models have the highest overall F1 and MCC, meaning their overall classification performance is stronger. Running both the SVM and MLP on a route proposal and flagging disagreements for human review would capture the benefits of both.

  \item \textbf{Monitor load factor and pricing targets closely.} The models confirm that load factor is the single most important predictor of profitability. Network planners should set minimum load factor thresholds per route category before committing to a schedule, with the model providing probabilistic support for edge cases.

  \item \textbf{Integrate the Streamlit dashboard into the route planning workflow.} The dashboard (\texttt{streamlit\_dashboard.py}) allows planners to input route characteristics and receive an immediate profitability prediction with probability scores across all five models. Making this tool available in the planning cycle reduces the reliance on analyst intuition for individual route decisions.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{subsec:limitations}

The most significant limitation is the synthetic component of the dataset. Although the base Kaggle records provide realistic financial relationships, the 18 added features and record augmentation introduce distributional assumptions that may not match real-world operational data. Specifically, competition index and passenger satisfaction score are the most speculative additions -- their relationship to route profitability in the dataset reflects our modelling choices rather than empirical measurement.

The FP cost assumption of £42,000 per incorrectly approved route is an estimate. The actual cost will vary substantially by route (a transatlantic slot is worth far more than a short-haul domestic route), and the model comparison would look different under alternative cost structures.

None of the models include temporal validation. We did not test whether a model trained on 2022--2023 data predicts 2024 profitability accurately. Airline economics can shift rapidly, and a model frozen on historical data may drift as fuel prices, competitive dynamics, and demand patterns change.

Finally, the black-box nature of the MLP and SVM means that individual predictions cannot be easily audited. For regulatory or governance reasons, an airline deploying these models in a formal planning process would likely need SHAP or LIME explanations to document why specific route proposals were flagged.

\subsection{Future work}
\label{subsec:future}

Three extensions would strengthen this analysis. First, incorporating SHAP values for the MLP and XGBoost would allow planner-facing explanations that identify which features drove each specific prediction. Second, testing the models on a held-out temporal partition (e.g.\ train on 2022--2023, test on 2024) would provide a more realistic picture of deployment performance. Third, stacking the five models into an ensemble meta-learner could squeeze out remaining performance gains, though the narrow inter-model gap suggests the benefit would be modest.

\subsection{Conclusion}
\label{subsec:conclusion}

Five machine learning classifiers were trained and evaluated on a 15,000-route airline profitability dataset. All five outperformed their Decision Tree baselines. The MLP produced the highest F1 (0.937) and MCC (0.827), while the SVM produced the lowest false positive cost (£1.76M), identifying loss-making routes with the highest precision of any model tested.

The consistent dominance of load factor and price per kilometre across all feature importance methods suggests that the fundamental economics of route profitability are not hidden -- they are simply not applied systematically in current planning processes. A machine learning screening tool built on these signals could meaningfully reduce the number of unprofitable routes that reach the schedule, with measurable financial benefits that compound over a full scheduling cycle.
